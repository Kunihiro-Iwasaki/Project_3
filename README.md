# Project3

## Title
### 78 Illegal Basements in Flood-Prone Areas of New York —Five Years After Hurricane Ida, Which Claimed 11 Lives
## Short description of what aimed to accomplish
#### Do you remember Hurricane Ida five years ago? Eleven people living in basement apartments died in floods caused by torrential rain. They were trapped when water poured in and were unable to escape in time. With the housing market in New York tight, there is still no shortage of people living in basement apartments, where rents are relatively low. As the risk of torrential rain increases due to climate change, I examined the disaster risks associated with basement apartments.
## Short description of findings
#### I overlaid New York City’s flood hazard map onto the 1,653 illegal basement apartments that the city identified as violations following Hurricane Ida in September 2021. The results revealed that 78 of these apartments are located in flood-prone areas. I also found that this number would rise to 103 at 2050 sea-level projections and to 547 at 2080 sea-level projections. This highlights how climate change is significantly increasing disaster risks.
## Summary of the data collection and analysis process
#### Using the “Illegal Basements” dataset from New York City’s Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD), I obtained location data and details of the violations for the 1,653 cases cited between September 1, 2021 (after Hurricane Ida) and July 8, 2026. I then overlaid this data with the “Stormwater Flood Maps”—flood hazard maps published by the New York City Department of Environmental Protection (DEP)—using GeoPandas. Flooding areas were projected based on the following scenarios: ① Rainfall of approximately 2 inches per hour (10% annual probability of occurrence) at current sea level ② Approximately 2 inches of rainfall per hour (10% annual probability of occurrence) at the 2050 sea level, with sea levels projected to rise by up to 2.5 feet; and ③ Approximately 3.5 inches of rainfall per hour (1% annual probability of occurrence) at the 2080 sea level, with sea levels projected to rise by up to 4.8 feet.
#### Additionally, for the 1,653 buildings, I used generative AI (GPT-5.4-mini) to classify the severity of the violations into four levels: “critical,” “high,” “middle,” and “low.” I used the facilities within the underground dwellings as the criteria for classification. Cases where plumbing was installed in walls or under floors, or where a full set of fixtures—such as showers, kitchens, and toilets—was installed, indicating they were intended for long-term living, were classified as “critical” or “high”; these two categories combined accounted for approximately 30 percent of the total.

## A section about what new skills, approaches, etc
#### I used GeoPandas to overlay vast amounts of polygon data and visualize it on a map. Since the charts created with GeoPandas did not look quite right, I converted them into a GeoPackage file, imported them into QGIS, and processed them there. With smartphone readability in mind, I used Illustrator to format the headings and annotations for the scrollable images.
